On 3 December, Prime Video brings back *Panchayat*, and with it the water tank, the peepal tree and the panchayat office in Phulera, a fictional village in Ballia, Uttar Pradesh. Over four seasons the show has grown from a lockdown curiosity into something close to a national habit. Ormax Media estimated that 28.2 million Indians watched Season 3 in the first half of 2024, making it the most-watched Hindi streaming series of that period.

It is, at heart, a show about an office. Its hero is a clerk. Its plots turn on files, registers, schemes, sanctions and the occasional missing signature. That makes it fair, and perhaps useful, to do what the show never does: open the actual records of India's gram panchayats and lay them beside Phulera.

Phulera is set in Ballia, so the fairest test is Uttar Pradesh's own records, and most of what follows draws on them. (The village on screen is in fact Mahodiya, in Sehore district of Madhya Pradesh, where the show is shot.) But the problems those records reveal are not Uttar Pradesh's alone. Where the national picture matters, it comes in too.

The comparison is not meant as a takedown. Fiction owes nothing to the census. But what a popular story chooses to leave out tells you something about the people it is made for, and *Panchayat* is made, above all, for the city.

## The secretary who has only one village

Abhishek Tripathi, the reluctant panchayat secretary, has one village to look after. He lives above the office. He has time, between solar-panel disputes and wedding feuds, to prepare for the CAT.

In Uttar Pradesh, that is the most fictional thing about him.

An undated report by the rural news outlet Gaon Connection, which is no longer online, put it starkly: about 58,000 gram panchayats, but only around 12,000 secretaries. One village development officer in Siddharth Nagar district told the paper he held charge of nine gram panchayats covering 13 villages. Secretaries in Maharajganj protesting against online attendance in 2026 said each of them was responsible for four or five panchayats. So the shortage is not a thing of the past. The state government is now preparing to recruit 13,116 panchayat secretaries in three phases.

Political scientists Rahul Verma and Nishant Ranjan, writing in *Seminar* in 2023 about what they call the "last-mile bureaucrat", found the same split nationally: southern and eastern states tend to have one secretary per panchayat, while northern states have one secretary covering several. By that measure, Phulera's one-village secretary looks less like Ballia than like the south. The real Sachiv ji of the Hindi heartland does not sit on the water tank watching the sunset. He is on a motorcycle between villages, carrying registers for a few dozen departmental duties.

The show's Vikas has a real counterpart too. Since 2021, Uttar Pradesh has placed a Panchayat Sahayak in each gram panchayat, recruited from the village itself. Recruitment notices put the honorarium at Rs 6,000 a month.

## The pradhan-pati is now a Government of India problem

Phulera's first great joke is constitutional. The pradhan's seat is reserved for a woman, so Manju Devi is the elected head. Her husband, Brij Bhushan Dubey, runs the panchayat. Everyone, including the block office, calls him Pradhan ji.

Audiences laughed. The Supreme Court did not. In 2023, on the Court's direction, the Ministry of Panchayati Raj set up an advisory committee under retired IAS officer Sushil Kumar to examine women pradhans being represented by male relatives. Its report, submitted in February 2025 and accepted by the government, recommended "exemplary penalties" for proven cases of proxy leadership. It also proposed helplines, watchdog committees and even annual awards for "anti-pradhan pati champions". The committee was set up for the whole country, not for one state. The Ministry treats proxy leadership as a national problem, and has asked every state government to act on it. Commentary on the report noted that the practice is most entrenched in the northern states, Uttar Pradesh among them.

To its credit, *Panchayat* lets Manju Devi grow. Writer Chandan Kumar has described reservation as a first step that can begin with women as rubber stamps and slowly give them exposure, networks and political capital. Manju Devi's arc follows that path closely.

Here is the fine print for Season 5, though. Manju Devi lost the election. The new pradhan is Kranti Devi, and the trailers make it plain who stands behind her: her husband Bhushan, the "Banrakas" of the memes. Phulera has replaced one pradhan-pati with another. The Centre now wants that arrangement penalised. The show still plays it for laughs.

## The seat that never comes round

The biggest gap between Phulera and the records concerns caste. Here the official numbers are hard to argue with.

When Uttar Pradesh fixed reservations for the 2021 panchayat elections, the state had 58,194 gram pradhan seats. Of these, 330 were reserved for Scheduled Tribes, 12,045 for Scheduled Castes and 15,712 for Other Backward Classes. That comes to 28,087 seats, roughly 48 per cent of all village headships in the state, reserved by caste before a single vote was cast. The seats also rotate. Under the state's formula, a seat reserved for a category in one election is not reserved for the same category in the next, so that reservation moves from village to village over the years.

Nor is this an Uttar Pradesh quirk. Article 243D of the Constitution, inserted by the 73rd Amendment, requires every state to reserve panchayat seats, and the posts of panchayat chairpersons, for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in proportion to their population, and at least one-third for women. States may add reservation for backward classes, as Uttar Pradesh does. Rotation of reserved seats from one village to another is built into the same article. A village that never meets the roster is unusual anywhere in India.

Phulera, in other words, is statistically unlikely to stay what it is. Across four seasons and two elections, its pradhans, its pradhan-patis, its up-pradhan, its MLA and even its villain carry upper-caste names. A critic writing for TwoCircles points out that the surnames of ordinary villagers are never revealed. In *Economic and Political Weekly*, Anisha George praised the show's eye for everyday rural life while calling its portrayal of caste unrealistic for a UP village. A 2026 article in the journal *Culture and Organization* described Phulera as "sanitized and nearly casteless."

None of this is accidental, and the makers do not pretend otherwise. Chandan Kumar told *Seminar* that keeping everyone from the same caste was a deliberate choice, meant to let viewers notice the absence and react to it.

Most viewers, the numbers suggest, have not reacted. The sociologist Satish Deshpande, in his essay "Caste and Castelessness" (*EPW*, 2013), argued that India's upper castes, having turned inherited privilege into education and professional careers, can afford to experience themselves as casteless, as though caste were something other people have. Phulera is a village built on that experience. It may be the main reason the show feels like home to so many people in flats in Noida and Pune.

## Why the city watches

That raises the question the records cannot answer by themselves: why does a show about a village do so well among people who live in cities?

Part of the answer lies in a census category. According to the Census, between 2001 and 2011 the number of census towns, settlements urban in character but still governed as villages, almost tripled, from 1,362 to 3,894. The line between urban and rural India has blurred, and a large part of the urban, salaried, subscription-paying audience is only a generation away from a Phulera. *Panchayat* is less the city looking at the village than the migrant middle class looking back at where it came from.

A second part lies in Abhishek himself. He is an engineer who missed placement, earns a fraction of his corporate friend's salary, and studies at night for an exam that might get him out. The anthropologist Craig Jeffrey, in *Timepass* (2010), wrote about exactly this figure among young men in Meerut: educated, underemployed, and waiting. For a generation of urban viewers caught between layoffs, entrance exams and insecure jobs, Abhishek is not a villager's story. He is theirs. The show quietly turns his waiting into belonging, and that is a deeply comforting story to watch at the end of a working day.

The third part is what the theorist Svetlana Boym called restorative nostalgia: rebuilding the lost home as it supposedly was. Phulera has mobile phones and solar panels, but its social fabric has been mended. Neighbours carry each other's grief, the funeral is attended by everyone, and conflict never curdles into violence. When Seasons 3 and 4 let real village politics in, with factions, a shooting and a bitter election, the reviews soured and Season 4's opening-week audience fell by about a quarter. The audience, it seems, did not come for realism. It came for repair.

## What the records leave out, and what they don't

  src={reelreal}

It would be easy to end by scolding a comedy for being a comedy. That is not the point. *Panchayat* has done something rare: it made tens of millions of urban Indians care about a level of government most of them could not have described before 2020. It turned the gram sabha, the BDO and the MLA's office into dinner-table conversation. Phrases like "Dekh raha hai Binod" now circulate in offices that have never seen a panchayat bhawan.

But Phulera is not a village. It is a portrait of what a certain India would like its villages to be: a single secretary with time to spare, a pradhan-pati whose meddling is endearing, and an electorate in which reservation touches gender and nothing else.

The real gram panchayats of Uttar Pradesh, and of much of India, look different. One secretary is stretched across nine villages. A Supreme Court-ordered committee wants pradhan-patis punished everywhere. And in Uttar Pradesh nearly half of all village headships go, by law, to Dalits, Adivasis and backward castes, rotating from one village to the next, election after election.

Season 5 arrives on 3 December. It will be worth watching for who rules Phulera. It will be worth watching just as closely for whose turn never seems to come.
