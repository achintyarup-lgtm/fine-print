Bengal tells the story of 1913 as a coronation. Stockholm’s own record tells a messier one: the Swedish Academy’s Nobel Committee did not want Tagore.

It recommended a French literary historian, Émile Faguet. The Academy as a whole — all eighteen members, of whom the committee is only a handful — overruled it. The Nobel Foundation lists only two such reversals in the prize’s history: Tagore in 1913 and Henri Bergson in 1927.

The vote turned on a Swedish poet who would win the prize himself three years later. The report that made Tagore’s case leaned on an Irishman’s preface. And a story that the Swedish royal house fixed the outcome, which still surfaces in scholarship, collapses on its own dates.

The reception was uglier than Bengal remembers. American papers greeted the first non-white laureate with open racial contempt. And Yeats, whose preface carried the vote, would later write: “Damn Tagore.”

## One nomination against ninety-seven

In 1913 the Swedish Academy received 32 nominations for 28 writers. The field included Thomas Hardy, Anatole France, Pierre Loti, Carl Spitteler and two Swedes, the explorer Sven Hedin and the poet Verner von Heidenstam.

Hardy came with the weight of 97 members of Britain’s Royal Society of Literature behind him. Tagore came with one name: the poet Thomas Sturge Moore, writing as an individual Fellow of that same society.

Tagore had never been nominated before. Winning in the year of a first nomination is rare in the prize’s history. His English *Gitanjali* had appeared from the India Society in London only in late 1912, with an introduction by W. B. Yeats. Macmillan’s edition followed in March 1913 and went through ten printings by November.

## The committee says Faguet

Two bodies are involved, and the whole story turns on the difference between them.

- **The Swedish Academy** is the full institution: eighteen members, elected for life. They vote on the prize, and the decision is theirs.
- **The Nobel Committee** is a small working group of three to five of those eighteen. It sifts the nominations, commissions expert assessments and recommends a winner.

In that era the committee sent the full Academy a single name, and the Academy usually confirmed it. The committee proposes; the Academy decides.

In 1913 the committee, chaired by the historian Harald Hjärne, settled on Émile Faguet, the French critic and moralist. The Academy, meeting in full, rejected that recommendation and chose Tagore instead.

The Nobel Foundation’s own history of the prize records this. It names only two exceptions to the rule that the Academy followed its committee: Tagore in 1913 and Bergson in 1927.

On 10 December 1913 it fell to Hjärne, whose committee had preferred Faguet, to deliver the presentation speech. He introduced the laureate as an “Anglo-Indian poet”.

The speech went further. Turning to the Brahmo Samaj, the reform movement Tagore grew up in, Hjärne described its unnamed founder (Rammohun Roy) as “an enlightened and influential man who had been much impressed by the doctrines of Christianity,” and said: “He endeavoured to give to the native Hindu traditions, handed down from the past, an interpretation in agreement with what he conceived to be the spirit and import of the Christian faith.” Stockholm’s first Asian laureate was introduced to Europe in Christian terms.

## A poet’s push, a borrowed preface

The man who turned the Academy was Verner von Heidenstam, then Sweden’s leading poet. He had read *Gitanjali* in translation, and literary historians credit his advocacy as the main reason Tagore won. Heidenstam was himself on the 1913 list of nominees. He won the prize in 1916.

The formal case came from the academician Per Hallström, whose report to the Academy is dated 29 October 1913. By his own admission, no biographical material had come with the nomination. He relied entirely on Yeats’s introduction to *Gitanjali*.

The Academy did have one member who could have read the Bengali. The orientalist Esaias Tegnér Jr knew the language. According to the Nobel Foundation’s own account, he made do with Tagore’s English translation.

The Bengali originals were within reach. According to a 2012 account in *Frontier*, *Naivedya*, *Kheya* and the Bengali *Gitanjali* reached the Nobel Library on 18 July 1913, months before the vote.

The citation says as much. It praised verse by which Tagore “has made his poetic thought, expressed in his own English words, a part of the literature of the West.” The Bengali original was not what Stockholm judged.

## “The Ignoble Decision”

Stockholm’s surprise was polite. America’s was not.

On 13 November the *New York Evening Post* called the award “the first time on record that the prize has been given to anybody but a white person.” Paper after paper repeated the line.

On 15 November the *Los Angeles Times* ran a column by Gordon Ray Young under the headline “The Ignoble Decision: Hindu Poet Unworthy of Nobel Prize”. It complained the prize had gone to “a Hindu poet whose name few people can pronounce, with whose work fewer in America are familiar, and whose claim for that high distinction still fewer will recognize.”

The same day the *New York Times* offered its consolation, misspelling him “Babindranath”. Tagore, it reasoned, “if not exactly one of us, is, as an Aryan, a distant relation of all white folk.”

Its first report had already set the tone. It called the award a most astonishing literary announcement and described Tagore as entirely unknown outside his own country.

## Yeats turns

The prize leaned on Yeats’s preface. Twenty-two years later, Yeats disowned the poet he had launched.

In a letter postmarked 7 May, probably 1935, he wrote to William Rothenstein, the painter who had first put Tagore’s translations in his hands:

> “Damn Tagore. We got out three good books, Sturge Moore and I, and then, because he thought it more important to see and know English than to be a great poet, he brought out sentimental rubbish and wrecked his reputation. Tagore does not know English, no Indian knows English.”

“Sturge Moore and I”: the other man who helped shape the English books was the poet who had nominated Tagore for the prize.

The letter does not end in contempt. Yeats goes on: “I shall return to the question of Tagore but not yet — I shall return to it because he has published, in recent [? years], and in English, prose books of great beauty, and these books have been ignored because of the eclipse of his reputation as a poet.” Allan Wade, who printed the letter in *The Letters of W. B. Yeats* (1954, pp. 834–835), worked from a typewritten copy and could not fix the year.

Yeats had won the Nobel himself in 1923. By then the Western cult of Tagore had faded, and the man who built it was among those walking away.

Rothenstein also undoes a myth still heard in Bengal: that Yeats rewrote *Gitanjali*. Rothenstein’s own account is that Yeats went through the translations carefully but respected Tagore’s English too much to make more than slight changes here and there.

Tagore credited Yeats more generously. In a letter Rothenstein printed in *Since Fifty* (1939, p. 112), he wrote: “Then came those delightful days when I worked with Yeats and I am sure the magic of his pen helped my English to attain some quality of permanence.”

## The prince who didn’t fix it

A darker version of the story has circulated for over a century. In it, the Swedish Crown pushed the prize after a visit to India by Prince Wilhelm, son of King Gustaf V.

It is old. A study in *International Yeats Studies* records that rumours ran through India after the award. One of them, said to have started in Sweden, held that the Academy chose Tagore under pressure from Prince William. The same study calls it erroneous.

It also survives. The Argentine historian Martín Bergel, writing on Tagore’s 1924 South American journey, repeats it, attributing it to “algunas versiones” (some versions) and citing a 1989 article about China’s campaign for the Nobel. That article is not an archival study of 1913.

The dates do not hold it up:

- Prince Wilhelm’s eastern voyage of 1911–12 ran via the Suez Canal and Ceylon to Siam, for the coronation festivities there.
- His travel book, *Där solen lyser* (Norstedts, 1913), is about the Siam visit. No source I found places him in India or with Tagore before the prize.
- The prince was not a member of the Swedish Academy. The prize was decided by its eighteen members, against their own committee’s advice.

There is a twist. A royal link to Tagore did exist, but it came later. In 1927 Norstedts published *Eldflugor*, Tagore’s *Fireflies*, in an authorised Swedish translation by Prince Wilhelm. Fourteen years after the rumour, the prince became Tagore’s translator.

Whether the two men met during Tagore’s visits to Sweden in 1921 and 1926 is still an open question.

## A stranger made a brother

Tagore did not go to Stockholm. At the banquet on 10 December 1913, his telegram was read by Mr Clive, the British Chargé d’Affaires: “I beg to convey to the Swedish Academy my grateful appreciation of the breadth of understanding which has brought the distant near, and has made a stranger a brother.”

The medal and diploma reached him in Calcutta, handed over by the Governor of Bengal, Lord Carmichael, at Government House on 29 January 1914.

When he finally came to Sweden in 1921, his host in Stockholm was Sven Hedin, one of the writers he had beaten in 1913. In 1926 he returned for a few days, met the Academy and read his poems on Swedish radio.

In 1935 the laureate used his own right to nominate. The Nobel archive lists Tagore as nominator of the Irish writer James H. Cousins.

The prize Bengal treats as destiny was a split decision. A committee said no, a rival poet said yes, and a report built on Yeats carried the room. None of that diminishes Tagore. It does mean the most famous decision in Bengali literary history was made by eighteen people who, by their own record, judged it in English.
