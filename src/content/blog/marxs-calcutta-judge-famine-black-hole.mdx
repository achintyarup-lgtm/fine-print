In 1872 a judge of the Calcutta High Court stood before the Bethune Society and explained how the Indian village had come to be. Nine years later, in his study in north London, Karl Marx copied out the judge’s argument and wrote beside it: “Dieser respectable Esel bildet sich ein” — “This respectable ass imagines.”

The remark sits in a notebook Marx filled between December 1880 and March 1881, now held at the International Institute of Social History (IISH) in Amsterdam. For more than a century it was known mainly to specialists, through Lawrence Krader’s 1972 edition. It went online in full, in Marx’s own mixture of German and English, only in 2023–24, in the historical-critical edition of his collected works, the MEGA.

Read together with other notebooks in the same archive, it shows something that has rarely been laid out in one place. Through his last decade and a half, much of what Marx knew about Bengal reached him through Calcutta: its judges, its journals, its learned societies and its newspapers. He was scathing about most of it.

## The judge

John Budd Phear sat on the Calcutta High Court from 1864 to 1876, then served as Chief Justice of Ceylon. In 1880 he published *The Aryan Village in India and Ceylon* in London. Its first part, on village life in Bengal, had already appeared in the *Calcutta Review* in 1874. Its third part, on the “evolution of the Indo-Aryan social and land system”, was the text of his 1872 Bethune Society lecture.

Marx excerpted all three parts, on pages 131–157 of the notebook the IISH catalogues as B_162. He also wrote a short note on his source. In the MEGA transcription it reads: “Dieser Bursch residirte 10 Jahre in Calcutta; lebte in Ceylon 1877–79. Das 3d Paper … was read 1872 durch den Burschen vor der Bethune Society of Calcutta.” (This fellow lived ten years in Calcutta; the third paper was read by the fellow before the Bethune Society of Calcutta.)

He used Phear for facts about the Bengal village, its headmen, its barbers and washermen, its moneylenders. Phear’s theories he would not accept. Where the judge described village arrangements as feudal, Marx wrote: “dieser Esel Phear nennt die Constitution des village feudal” (this ass Phear calls the village constitution feudal). Where Phear sketched an imagined village instead of a real one, Marx wrote that Phear “hätte besser gethan statt seines hypothetischen Kohls description solcher instances zu geben!” (would have done better to describe real instances instead of his hypothetical drivel). And against Phear’s claim that a ladder of rank grew up inside the village from the start, he wrote: “Dieser respectable Esel bildet sich ein … (Der asinus lässt auch alles durch private families gründen)” (This respectable ass imagines … The ass has everything founded by private families too).

The target was not only the judge. In the same pages Marx copied a passage Phear had quoted from “a young zemindar, Babu Peary Chund Mookerjee”, read to the Bengal Social Science Association. The zamindar wrote that “A husbandman of the present day is the primitive being he always (!) has been.” The exclamation mark in brackets is Marx’s.

Marx’s sharpest note on Bengal concerns the Permanent Settlement of 1793. That year Lord Cornwallis fixed the land revenue for ever and turned the zamindars, until then revenue collectors, into proprietors of the soil. Reading Phear’s account of how rights in land multiplied under them, Marx wrote:

> “Die Verwandlung – durch die English rogues and asses – der Zemindars in private proprietors machte eo ipso (wenn auch nicht in Idee jener asses) all intermediate interests zu rights in land.”

In English: turning the zamindars into private proprietors, a work of “English rogues and asses”, by that very act made every intermediate interest a right in land, “even if those asses had no such idea”.

He had already noted the moment of the decision a year or two earlier, in a chronology of Indian history he compiled in 1879–80 from Robert Sewell and Mountstuart Elphinstone. The ruling, he wrote, was “promulgated at Calcutta in March 1793 zum Jubel der überraschten Zemindars!” (to the jubilation of the astonished zamindars!).

The same notebook carries Marx’s excerpts from the Russian historian Maksim Kovalevsky on communal landownership. Kovalevsky leaned heavily on Calcutta sources, the *Calcutta Review* above all, and on a paper the Calcutta missionary James Long read to the Bengal Social Science Association in 1872, comparing village communities in India and Russia. Long is better remembered in Bengal for going to prison over the English translation of *Nil Darpan*. On British land policy Marx added his own verdict: “Die engl. Esel brauchten immense Zeit um die wirklichen Conditions des Grundbesitzes … auch nur approximativ zu begreifen” (the English asses took an immense time to grasp even approximately the real conditions of landholding).

## The famine

Calcutta reached Marx’s notebooks well before Phear did. In May 1867, months before the first volume of *Capital* appeared, Parliament ordered the printing of a Blue Book on the Bengal and Orissa famine of 1866. Marx worked through it in a notebook he kept from 1868, which the IISH catalogues as B_112, under the heading “Return. East India (Bengal and Orissa Famine). Ordered by the H. o. Commons to be Printed 31 May 1867”.

These pages have not yet been published in the MEGA, so the readings below are this writer’s own, from the manuscript. Marx’s notes are mostly headings with page references, but they show where he was looking. He flagged two Calcutta sources: an article on the starving poor of Orissa from the *Englishman*, “(Calcutta)”, of 10 May 1866, and the *Calcutta Gazette* of 3 August 1866.

He followed the grain trade. His headings include “Effects of Exportation of Grain” and “Export of Rice from Orissa”. He noted demand for “jute, oil seeds etc., hence decrease of breadth of land under rice”, and a “Rise of Prices, not accompanied by Rise of Wages”.

He followed official doctrine too. Against one page he wrote “Rules of Pol. Economy”, and against another “Polit. Econ.” with the name of the Lieutenant-Governor of Bengal, Cecil Beadon. He noted the official line, “No interference with legitimate trade”, and a few lines later the report’s own finding: “There was no trade to be interfered with.” Elsewhere he wrote simply: “Red tape and Famine”.

He also copied out a passage in which the Blue Book’s authors conceded the cost of the new order. Modern means of communication, they wrote, drained grain from districts where it was plentiful “to an extent which probably did not occur in the old days of native hoarding”. Marx underlined those words.

## The Black Hole

In his 1879–80 chronology of Indian history Marx reached June 1756. Siraj-ud-Daula had taken Fort William, and the British prisoners were locked overnight in the guardroom. Marx set the episode down in a few lines:

> “… die 146 men, (accidentally it seems) aber crushed into a room 20 feet square u. mit but one small window; am andern Morgen (von Holwell selbst das Zeug erzählt) nur noch 23 am Leben … Dies „das Black Hole of Calcutta“, worüber die English hypocrites so viel sham scandal bis dato machen.”

In English: the 146 men were “accidentally, it seems” crushed into a room twenty feet square with one small window. Next morning (“Holwell himself tells this stuff”) only 23 were alive. “This is ‘the Black Hole of Calcutta’, over which the English hypocrites have made so much sham scandal to this day.”

Each of Marx’s asides carries weight. He recorded that Siraj had ordered the captives kept safe until morning, and he took the deaths to be an accident, not an atrocity. He noted that the story came from John Zephaniah Holwell, one of the survivors. Historians argued over Holwell’s figures for much of the twentieth century. Marx had voiced the doubt privately, decades earlier, and aimed his scorn at the British use of the episode rather than at the dead.

The same chronology is sharper still on the British in Calcutta. When a treasure ship from the new Nawab Mir Jafar arrived late in 1757, Marx wrote that it “entzückt die Calcutta »Hörner«”. It delighted the Calcutta blockheads. The MEGA editors note that the word is Marx’s own, not his source’s.

## The letter from Calcutta

The traffic did not run only one way. On 15 August 1871, the General Council of the International Working Men’s Association met in London with Hermann Jung in the chair. Marx and Engels were among those present. The minute book, signed by the secretary, John Hales, survives in the IISH. On page 273 it records:

> “He also read a letter from Calcutta asking for powers to start a section in India. The Secretary was instructed to write, and advise the establishment of a branch, but he to inform the writer that it must be self supporting. He was also to urge the necessity of enrolling natives in the Association.”

A London weekly, the *Eastern Post*, printed part of the letter on 19 August. It said that “great discontent exists amongst the people” and that the International “would bring the mass of the people into its organisation if a section was started”. The writer’s name has never been established. The letter itself does not appear in the IISH inventories of the Jung papers or of Marx and Engels’s papers. Marx’s own summary of the Council’s minutes for that meeting mentions new branches in Liverpool and Loughborough, and says nothing about Calcutta.

## What he never wrote

Put together, the notebooks show Marx reading Bengal through Calcutta for a decade and a half: a famine Blue Book and the *Calcutta Gazette* in the late 1860s; the *Calcutta Review*, the Bengal Social Science Association and a Bethune Society lecture in 1879–81. His own money-market notes even record the city’s population at the census of January 1866: 377,924.

Yet he never wrote about Calcutta itself. Its streets, its Bengali society, the arguments of its own reformers do not appear. In his notes the city is the seat of British power, the place where decisions were “promulgated” and where the “Hörner” lived. The single line on how its people lived comes from Phear: in Calcutta and other very large towns, joint families of 300 to 400 people, servants included, live under one roof. The one Bengali voice in these pages, Peary Chund Mookerjee, reached Marx inside an English judge’s book, and Marx answered it with an exclamation mark.

In 1871 someone in Calcutta wrote to the organisation Marx led, asking to join. Ten years later Marx was reading a Calcutta judge and calling him an ass. The two never met on the page.
