## A dance, and a piece of news

On the evening of 17 January 1956, a group of Indian journalists sat watching a tribal dance at Along. Along was then a small administrative post in the Siang Frontier Division of NEFA, the North East Frontier Agency. Today the region is Arunachal Pradesh, and Along is called Aalo.

The journalists were there as guests of the government. They were on a guided press tour of a frontier that few Indians had seen, a land of steep forested hills, fast rivers and villages a week’s walk from the nearest road. The tour was meant to show the nation that its new administration was reaching these remote valleys.

Then, in the middle of the dance, an officer turned to a colleague with a piece of news so strange that he seems to have treated it half as a joke.

A snowman, he said, had been killed.

The officer was Captain U. Chakma, who was acting as the Political Officer of the division. He had heard the story only a few hours earlier. He did not mean to announce it. But one of the reporters was listening.

The next morning the reporter, Prem Bhatia, filed his story from Pasighat. “A startled Indian official broke the news this morning that a snowman has been killed about 120 miles from Along,” it began. The creature, he wrote, was “10 feet high, man-like, white and hairless.” It had been killed by Bokar hunters who had gone out after a takin, a large, shaggy wild animal of the high slopes.

On 18 January 1956, The Statesman printed it under the headline “Snowman Reported Killed.”

What followed fills most of an 86-page file, now kept in the National Archives of India among the papers of Jairamdas Daulatram, then Governor of Assam. It is stamped Confidential. On its cover, a clerk has written its subject in a neat, sober hand: Papers relating to “Snowman”.

## A rumour on foot

Where had the story come from? Chakma’s own report, written a few weeks later, traces it back step by step. It is a lesson in how news travelled in the hills: on foot, mouth to ear, gaining a little with every mile.

On 17 January a man named Neog called at Chakma’s office. He was a compounder, a medical assistant, posted at a remote outpost called Tadadege, and he was going home on leave. Chakma, as he always did, was quizzing him about affairs in the outposts. Almost in passing, Neog mentioned something he had heard.

Neog had not seen anything himself. He had heard it from a group of traders of the Bokar tribe, while he was camped at a place called Yapuik, six days’ march from Along. The traders said that around 30 December 1955 a creature had been killed somewhere between Tadadege and Tato.

The description was remarkable. The creature lived in the snow. It looked like a man. It was white, and hairless except for hair on its head. It was bigger than a man, more than ten feet tall. And it was not a bear or a monkey, the traders insisted, because the Bokars knew bears and monkeys very well.

Nobody knew what had happened to the body. Perhaps the hunters had eaten it. Perhaps they had kept it for a sacrifice.

Chakma acted at once. On 18 January a wireless message went from Along to the Adviser’s office in Shillong, the capital of Assam. Its clipped telegraph language still crackles with excitement:

> “Report received from traders of Bokar tribe at Yapik one abominable snowman killed near Tadadege (.) Description (.) Hands and legs like man (.) Colour white (.) Whole body hairless except white hair on the head (.) Huge figure (.) Height ten to fifteen feet.”

Notice how the creature grew. In Neog’s account it was more than ten feet tall. In the telegram it was ten to fifteen.

A second message went to the officer at Mechukha, the nearest outpost. “This report if correct is of world importance,” it said. The officer was to investigate in person and to preserve the body “by every means”, if it had not already been eaten. He was also warned to take precautions, because the local people were deeply superstitious about this creature. They believed that if its footprints were found near a village, the villagers would start dying in an epidemic.

## Delhi wants to know

The timing could hardly have been worse, or better, depending on your point of view. The world was in the grip of yeti fever. In 1951 the mountaineer Eric Shipton had photographed giant footprints in the snow near Everest, and newspapers everywhere had printed them. In 1954 the London Daily Mail had sent a full expedition to Nepal to hunt for the creature. Now, it seemed, Indian tribesmen had simply gone out and killed one.

New Delhi reacted quickly. On 2 February 1956 the Ministry of External Affairs, which then looked after NEFA, wrote to Shillong marked IMMEDIATE. It enclosed the newspaper cutting and asked whether there was “any truth in the report.”

The letter’s subject line is one of the finest pieces of official prose I have read. It reads: Alleged murder of a Snowman in NEFA.

Murder. Not killing, not hunting, not shooting. Somewhere in the ministry a clerk had decided that the victim was, if not quite a person, then close enough to deserve the word.

Parliament joined in. A member, Shri Velayudhan, gave notice of a starred question to the Prime Minister in the Lok Sabha. He wanted to know whether the Political Officer had reported that a Bokar tribesman had killed an “abominable snow man” near Tadadege, and what details the government had. Another member, Shri Sivamurthi Swami, went further. He asked whether arrangements had been made to preserve the snowman’s body, and what steps the Department of Anthropology had taken to examine it.

The Department of Anthropology in Calcutta had indeed come knocking. It asked the frontier administration to send out an expedition. Shillong politely told it to wait until the facts were clearer.

For a few weeks, then, a dead snowman lay somewhere in the hills, in the imagination of officials, journalists and members of Parliament. All that was needed was for someone to go and look at it.

## The captain’s Dimee

Captain Chakma, it turns out, was not a man to dismiss such a story. In a long report sent to Shillong in February 1956, he confessed his own interest openly.

“Since my posting in this Division,” he wrote, “I myself have been very much interested in the snow man gossip and other spiritual beliefs of these tribes.” If he had not been buried under “boggies of papers” at headquarters, he said, he would already have written several articles on the subject. Then he added a sentence few officials would put on file: “I personally believe that snow man is really in existance.”

The local people, he explained, called it Dimee Letu or Dimee Joge. It was mostly invisible. It could change its size, appearing sometimes large and sometimes small.

Chakma had a tidy, almost scientific explanation for this. The creature was white, he reasoned, so against the snow it could not be seen at all. Hunters saw only its shadow. And a shadow grows and shrinks with the angle of the sun.

He also passed on a chilling story from a village headman at Miging. In 1949, the headman said, a man named Ringa from a Tibetan village near the border had led a party with ponies over the high hills above the Tsigong valley. On the last night of their journey, a horde of Dimee attacked them. Most of the party were killed. Ringa escaped with one or two men, badly wounded, and later died. His son, the traders said, was still alive.

At another village, Chakma watched a ritual to please the Dimee. In local belief, the Dimee was the spirit that owned the wild animals of the high snows: the takin, the musk deer and the snow bear. A different spirit, Yapom, owned the animals of the lowland jungle. So when hunters went after a takin, they were taking from the Dimee’s own herd.

The report ends with a very human touch. Much of this material, Chakma said, was going into a book he was writing. Could Shillong please keep it out of the press until his book was approved?

The man who had leaked the snowman to the newspapers was now anxious that nobody leak his footnotes.

## Five miles into the forest

The man who finally went to look was T. Tsering, the Assistant Political Officer at Mechukha. From the start he had doubted the story. As early as 20 January he had signalled that the only animal recently killed near Tadadege was a bear, about two months earlier.

He made the journey anyway. On 2 March 1956, writing from his camp at Manigong, he reported what he had found.

He had gone to Tadadege and found the man who had laid the trap, a villager named Shingtung Jenpen. Shingtung led him about five miles from the village into the forest, to the very spot. Tsering examined the trap and the ground around it. He collected what was left: some bones and some hair.

The bones were limb bones. The hair was black, “very much like that of bear.” There was no skull. According to Shingtung, the animal had been trapped months earlier, in the Bokar month the officer wrote down as Narbin. A follow-up wireless message from Along put it in September.

So the white, hairless, fifteen-foot man of the snows had turned out to be a black, hairy bear caught in a forest trap in the autumn.

Tsering also learned something about the Bokars’ own view of the matter. Dimi, they told him, was simply their name for the snowman. But nobody in living memory had ever killed one, and nobody had ever actually seen one. It was, in his words, “only hear and say.”

As for how the rumour started, the people of Tadadege had a theory. Two men from a village called Poying, named Kapor and Urgin, had passed through Tadadege some time after the bear was trapped. They, it was suggested, had spread the tale.

Tsering’s conclusion was blunt. “I request no further search of snowman is necessary,” he wrote, “and these are all illmory gossips.”

The bones and hair were sent down from the mountains by special runner, to be examined in Shillong. The government’s reply to Parliament, drafted on 3 March by Lt. Col. R. Yusuf Ali, the Deputy Adviser, put it politely. On present information, it said, “the whole report is a rumour of a type common in the tribal areas.”

## A quarrel on file

That might have been the end of it. Instead, the file comes alive, because now the officials began to argue among themselves.

On 13 March 1956, K.L. Mehta, the senior official handling the file in Shillong, wrote a note that still stings. “This was obviously a hoax,” he began. “I am surprised that anyone in Poying village could have heard about the snow man at all.” Then came the rebuke. Captain Chakma “should not have succumbed to the temptation of giving premature publicity to this report at Along!” The exclamation mark is his.

Chakma replied the very next day, and his note is a small masterpiece of injured dignity.

First, he protested, he had never meant to publicise anything. During the press party’s visit he and the Deputy Adviser had been chatting about “certain joking news,” and it reminded him of the snowman report he had received only a few hours earlier. “While I was giving out this to DAG, one reporter overheard us. I have been dragged on to the turmoil inadvertently.”

Second, he did not trust Tsering. He suspected the officer had made up his mind before he set out. Tsering, he wrote, had not wanted to go to Tadadege at all. He had wanted to go down to Margherita to settle some family matters. And there had been two murders around the new outpost since it opened, so the place felt hostile.

Third, Chakma had a new witness. On 27 February a Bokar had come to Along and told him more. The creature, this man said, did look like a bear. But it had only a slight patch of hair on its chest and back. Its forelegs were more like human hands than a bear’s paws. Its face was like a man’s, only slightly longer.

And there was the matter of the palm. Bokars, Chakma explained, love bear meat. Yet they did not eat this animal, because they suspected it was human. So they cut off its right palm at the wrist and carried it to the village for a ritual called Peepe-Roksing. They removed the skin and studied the veins, as an omen. The veins confirmed that the creature was a “man” who lived mostly in the snows.

Someone in Shillong read this paragraph with a raised eyebrow. In the margin, in pencil, is a note: the Bokar had not seen the creature himself, and was only repeating what others had told him. Hearsay, in other words, about hearsay.

Chakma was not finished. Perhaps, he suggested, the snowman was an extinct kind of bear, a “missing link” in the chain of human evolution, “well compared with Java man.” He asked that a qualified officer or an anthropologist look into the matter properly. It might, he wrote, be “a worthy contribution to scientific advancement.”

Then, almost in the same breath, he took “the golden opportunity” to submit a sketchy draft of his own on the local tribes, and offered more.

## The fear beneath the fur

It would be easy to laugh at Captain Chakma: the leak, the ten-foot shadow, the palm, Java man. But buried in his defensive note is a paragraph that changes the whole story.

Forget the snowman, he wrote. Ask a different question. People in these hills kill bears all the time. Why, on this particular occasion, did some Bokars start talking about a Dimi?

His answer is worth quoting at length. The government had recently opened a new administrative centre at Manigong, in the heart of Bokar country. “The local tribes are expecting bad omens,” Chakma wrote. “They anticipate famine, death, diseases and other effects of ‘evil eyes’.” To them, he suggested, the arrival of the administration might feel like killing a Dimi, an act that “brings fear of extinction of the tribe.”

And so his conclusion, underlined in the original: a “certain amount of hostility brewing in their mind against us.”

The Bokar belief, remember, was that whoever killed a Dimi would be punished with disease and death. If anyone even claimed to have seen a Dimi’s footprint, the whole village would suffer epidemic and disaster. A story about a Dimi being killed was therefore not a zoological report. It was a story about catastrophe arriving.

What is striking is that the senior officers in Shillong did not brush this aside. Lt. Col. Yusuf Ali, who only days earlier had called the whole thing a rumour, now wrote that these points “ring very true and should certainly be investigated.”

Then he wrote something rarely found in an official file. “In some areas, one has an uneasy suspicion that the fears have been justified,” he admitted, “and that the advent of the administration has also brought in economic stresses, and diseases among hitherto vigorous tribes.” The Siang valley, he wrote, was a case in point, compared with the “virtually untouched” Bori area.

He added a quieter, sharper observation about the murders that so worried officers in new outposts. “We may overestimate murders in a new area,” he wrote. “They have probably continued for a long time, but are only now coming to notice.” In other words, the violence might not be new. Only the government’s gaze was.

He also asked a fine question about the bones. If the animal really had been a Dimi, would the people not have reacted when an officer came and carried its remains away? Their silence was itself a kind of evidence.

The snowman was a bear. But the fear that dressed the bear in a snowman’s skin was real, and two officers of the new Indian state saw it clearly, if only for a moment, on paper.

## The Governor’s snowman

The file holds one more surprise. Its older pages, from 1952 to 1954, show that the most enthusiastic snowman hunter in Assam was the Governor himself.

It began in May 1952. Hipshon Roy, then the Assistant Political Officer at Mechukha, was touring near the Lola Pass, a high route into Tibet at about 14,000 feet. Some 500 feet below the pass, he came upon a line of footprints in the snow. The headmen of a nearby village called Lalung looked at them and said at once what they were.

The people of the border, Roy reported, knew two different creatures and never confused them. One was the Drimo, a giant bear. The other was the Mirgoe, the snowman. The name, he was told, came from Tibetan: mir for man, goe for strong.

The last Mirgoe had been seen about twelve years earlier. A Monpa trader travelling to Tibet had camped at the Lola Pass. He went out to shoot some birds for his dinner, and when he came back he saw a very hairy man trying to open his food bag. He fired a poisoned arrow. The creature fled, and later the trader heard groaning from behind the hill. But the shock was too much for him. He turned back, fell ill in his village, and died.

The Governor’s office was fascinated. In February 1953 it sent a questionnaire to the frontier, asking for names, places, heights and altitudes. Some questions were sensible: who had seen the creature, and exactly where? Others went somewhere stranger. Local stories said the female snowman had long breasts that got in her way when she walked downhill, so she threw them over her back to climb. The questionnaire asked, with complete seriousness: “Who has seen the female snow man putting her breasts on her back?” Someone later added, by hand, a twelfth question: “Does the snowman eat yaks?”

The Governor also wrote to an old friend. Mirabehn, the English disciple of Mahatma Gandhi, was then living at an ashram in Tehri-Garhwal, in the hills of Uttar Pradesh. She had already told the newspapers about villagers there who had seen giant footprints in their fields. One old man described seeing, by moonlight, a figure “very tall with great hairy shoulders, as if wearing a big shaggy fur coat.”

Mirabehn replied warmly, enclosing her article. She ended it with a plea that could have been written yesterday: that no one should shoot or kill this “very timid” creature, this “unknown brother of the mountains.”

In January 1954 the Governor dictated an article of his own, “Snowman in the Abor Hills.” He concluded that the creature was certainly not a man, but “equally unlikely to be merely a bear or a ‘Langur Monkey’.” He ended with a flight over the frontier, close enough to see the snow-capped Lola Pass “beckoning us most tantalizingly.” From that height, he wrote, the snowman was safe from prying eyes, “and certainly safe from any poisoned arrows, which we did not possess.”

## What the file keeps

Read from front to back, the file tells a comic story. A rumour walks down a mountain on the feet of traders. It grows taller at every stop. A captain repeats it at a dance, a reporter overhears it, and within a fortnight a ministry in Delhi is enquiring into the “alleged murder of a Snowman.” Members of Parliament ask whether the body is being preserved. Then a reluctant officer walks five miles into a forest and finds a few bear bones.

But read it again and other things come into view.

There is a Governor in Shillong, a busy man running a huge and difficult province, who still finds time to write to Mirabehn about footprints in the snow. There is a questionnaire that solemnly asks about a female snowman’s breasts. There are officers who take local belief seriously, sometimes too seriously, and others who dismiss it too fast. There is a pencil note in a margin, quietly pointing out that a key witness saw nothing.

And there is that one paragraph about fear. For the Bokars, a new government outpost was arriving in their valley, with officers and clerks and new rules. A story about a forbidden killing, and the punishment that must follow, may have been one way of talking about that. The officials did not fully understand the language. But for a moment, two of them heard it.

The snowman, as far as this file is concerned, was a bear. The fear was not imaginary. It is still sitting there, in a confidential folder, waiting for anyone who cares to read past the fur.
