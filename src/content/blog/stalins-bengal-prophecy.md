## A forecast in the Kremlin

On 9 February 1951, Stalin told four leaders of the Communist Party of India that the Bengali provinces would be the first to break away from Pakistan. Twenty years later, in 1971, East Pakistan became Bangladesh.

The meeting itself is well documented. It ran for more than three hours. But the most widely cited English translation, on the Wilson Center Digital Archive, does not render the Bengal sentence as a forecast.

The original Russian record, kept in Stalin's personal papers at the Russian State Archive of Socio-Political History (RGASPI), says something sharper. This piece sets out what the Russian says, what the translation lost, and what else the Soviet record shows about how Stalin saw Bengal and the partition.

## One verb, two meanings

The Russian sentence uses a future-tense verb: the Bengali provinces "will fall away" from Pakistan. The Wilson Center's English translation renders it as a present-tense description.

The Russian, as quoted from the archive file by the Moscow historian Valeriy Kashin, reads:

> «До чего этот искусственный раздел видно хотя бы по Бенгалии. Бенгальские провинции отпадут от Пакистана в первую очередь»

A literal translation: "What this artificial partition has led to can be seen if only from Bengal. The Bengali provinces will fall away from Pakistan first."

The English version on the Wilson Center Digital Archive, translated by Gary Goldberg, reads:

> "What this artificial partition has led to is evident from Bengal alone. In the first place, the Bengali provinces are disconnected from Pakistan."

|  | Russian original (via Kashin) | Vijay Singh, 2006 | Wilson Center (Goldberg) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Verb | «отпадут» (will fall away, will break off) | "will fall away" | "are disconnected" |
| Tense | Future | Future | Present |
| «в первую очередь» | first, before other parts | "at the first opportunity" | "in the first place" |
| What it says | A prediction | A prediction | A description |

An earlier English translation agrees with the Russian. Vijay Singh translated the same RGASPI file for Delhi's *Revolutionary Democracy* (Vol. XII, No. 2, September 2006). His version reads: "This artificial division is clear particularly in Bengal. The province of Bengal will fall away from Pakistan at the first opportunity."

Singh's text slips elsewhere. It renders the preceding sentence as "the people have doubts about them", where Kashin's Russian has «народ сомнет их», the people will crush them, as Goldberg has it. Each English version errs once; the Russian settles both.

Kashin reads it the same way. He writes that Stalin «предсказал» (predicted) it, citing RGASPI, fond 558, opis 11, delo 310, sheet 75. His article appeared in *Vostok (Oriens)*, the journal of the Institute of Oriental Studies, Moscow, in 2024 (No. 3).

The English record notes it was taken down by V. Grigor'yan and dated 10 February 1951.

The Russian original itself settles the matter. The typed record, scanned and published alongside the English on the Wilson Center Digital Archive, carries the sheet number 75 in the corner. The sentence reads exactly as Kashin quotes it: «Бенгальские провинции отпадут от Пакистана в первую очередь».

## The road to that sentence

The 1951 forecast fits a line of thinking that runs back a quarter of a century. Three earlier markers survive.

**1925: India as many nations.** Speaking to students of the Communist University of the Toilers of the East in Moscow on 18 May 1925, Stalin said: "Today, India is spoken of as a single whole. But there can scarcely be any doubt that, in the event of a revolutionary upheaval in India, scores of hitherto unknown nationalities, having their own separate languages and separate cultures, will appear on the scene." Bengal is not named.

**1947: the map.** Kashin reports that, looking at a map of the two new dominions in 1947, Stalin remarked: «Такое государство не может долго существовать» ("Such a state cannot exist for long"). Pakistan's two wings lay about 1,500 km apart across Indian territory. Kashin adds that staff of the Soviet foreign ministry's South-East Asia department often repeated the remark.

This one needs care. Kashin's source is *The Mitrokhin Archive II* (Andrew and Mitrokhin, 2005, p. 347), which rests on a KGB archivist's notes, not a transcript. Check that page before quoting it as Stalin's words.

**1948–52: the Soviet academic line.** A.M. Dyakov, the leading Soviet Indologist of the period, wrote in 1948 that partition «не разрешил ни одной проблемы индийской общественно-политической жизни» ("did not solve a single problem of Indian socio-political life"). On Bengal, he wrote that the Bengalis «оказались разделенными между двумя доминионами» ("found themselves divided between two dominions"). Their main economic and cultural centre, Calcutta, went to India; the districts producing raw materials and food went to Pakistan.

The Russian historian Larisa Chereshneva, in her 2009 doctoral dissertation, judges that Dyakov «выразил официальную позицию тогдашнего руководства СССР» ("expressed the official position of the Soviet leadership of the time").

## "An act of fraud" — and what reached the party

![Illustration: a hand in a dark suit sleeve draws a knife along the border between West Bengal and East Bengal on a map of the subcontinent, with both wings of Pakistan in red.](/images/stalin-bengal/partition-cut.webp)
*Stalin called the partition a swindle organised by the British. Illustration: AI-generated for The Fine Print.*

In the same conversation, Stalin called the partition a British swindle and told the Indian communists to campaign for reuniting the subcontinent. His remark on Bengal was offered as proof.

The Wilson Center English has: "As regards the partition of India, that this was an act of fraud organized by the British." The Russian original has «жульническая штука, организованная англичанами»: a swindling, fraudulent thing organised by the English.

Stalin then told the party to demand "a union between Pakistan, India, and Ceylon, \[both\] military and economic". He went on: "It will end with these three countries uniting... The leaders of Pakistan and Ceylon will oppose it but the people will crush them."

The union demand reached the party. Paragraph 19 of the CPI's secret Tactical Line, adopted in April 1951 and drafted "in consultation with the leaders of the CPSU", calls for "a policy of friendship and close alliance between India, Pakistan and Ceylon". The Bengal forecast does not appear anywhere in that document.

There is an irony here. In 1942 the CPI had backed the Pakistan demand through the Adhikari thesis, built on Stalin's own 1913 definition of a nation. It recast the demand as self-determination for "Muslim nationalities (i.e. Bihari, Bengali, Pathan etc)". Nine years later, Stalin called the outcome a fraud.

## Pakistan: courted, then written off

![Illustration: an unopened envelope with a red wax seal lies on an airfield runway beside sacks of grain and bales of jute, as a propeller airliner climbs away.](/images/stalin-bengal/pakistan-invitation.webp)
*Moscow invited Liaquat Ali Khan in 1949; he went to Washington instead, in May 1950. Illustration: AI-generated for The Fine Print.*

By February 1951 Stalin had already tried Pakistan and given up on it. That shift explains why a forecast of Pakistan's break-up could be said so coolly.

Kashin's 2024 article on Stalin and Pakistan, drawn from the Soviet foreign ministry archive (AVPRF), records these steps:

- In 1948–49 the USSR supplied Pakistan with 124,000 tonnes of wheat and bought its cotton and raw jute. Pakistan's jute was grown in East Bengal.
- On 2 June 1949 Stalin signed an invitation to Prime Minister Liaquat Ali Khan to visit Moscow. Kashin notes the document itself has not been found in Russian archives.
- Liaquat went to the United States instead, in May 1950.
- A foreign ministry note of 19 January 1950 to Vyshinsky had already judged that his interest in Moscow was meant mainly to pressure Britain over Kashmir and money.

By 2 March 1951, in his second meeting with the CPI leaders, Stalin was warning that Pakistan was the threat. Kashin quotes the Russian record: «Пакистан может Индию покорить. В Пакистане много реакционеров» ("Pakistan may subjugate India. There are many reactionaries in Pakistan").

Later in 1951, a Soviet foreign ministry telegram to Vyshinsky, authorised by Stalin, attacked Anglo-American "support against India to a more reactionary Pakistan" on Kashmir. Delhi's *Revolutionary Democracy* published the text, as reported by Dawn in 2008.

## Ekushey and the Urdu remark

![Illustration: sandals, open books and scattered papers lie on an empty road under a krishnachura tree in red bloom, with a colonial-era university building behind.](/images/stalin-bengal/ekushey-dawn.webp)
*Ekushey: Dhaka, February 1952. Illustration: AI-generated for The Fine Print.*

On 17 February 1953, four days before the first anniversary of the 21 February Bhasha Andolan shootings in Dhaka, Stalin told India's ambassador that Urdu could not be a real national language. It was among his last recorded conversations with a foreign diplomat. He died on 5 March.

**What happened on 21 February 1952.** Students gathered at Dhaka University in defiance of Section 144, demanding that Bengali be made a state language of Pakistan alongside Urdu. Police opened fire near the East Bengal Legislative Assembly. Among those killed were Abdus Salam, Rafiq Uddin Ahmed, Abul Barkat and Abdul Jabbar. A general strike followed across Dhaka.

Bengali became the second state language of Pakistan on 29 February 1956. In 1999 UNESCO made 21 February International Mother Language Day.

**What Stalin said a year later.** The fullest record is K.P.S. Menon's secret letter of 19 February 1953 to Foreign Secretary R.K. Nehru, held at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library. Menon writes that Stalin "asked whether it was true that Pakistan was evolving a language of its own". Menon told him Urdu had grown as a camp language and Pakistan was adding Persian and Arabic words to it. Stalin replied: "In that case, it cannot be a real national language."

In the same talk Stalin asked whether India had considered a federation with Pakistan: "That would be the ideal solution." When Menon spoke of the bitterness the British had sown, Stalin interjected: "How primitive it is... to create and run a State on the basis of religion!" Told that religion was entering Pakistan's constitution, with mullahs given a say on legislation, he said: "How absurd it all is!"

His interest in Pakistan's language was not new. Meeting Ambassador S. Radhakrishnan on 14 January 1950, Stalin had asked about Pakistan and "about language they spoke there".

**What the record does not say.** Neither Menon's letter nor the 1950 telegram mentions Bengal, Bengali or Dhaka. There is no evidence that Stalin knew of the Bhasha Andolan or had it in mind. Kashin reports that the Soviet record of the Menon meeting could not be found in the Russian archives. Every account therefore comes from Menon himself: his secret letter, and the version he published ten years later in his memoir *The Flying Troika* (1963). An outside trace exists too. On 19 February 1953 the American chargé in Moscow, Jacob Beam, cabled Washington that Menon had told him part of the talk was a "discussion of language problems in Russia and India". The cable does not mention Pakistan or Urdu.

The juxtaposition is the reader's to weigh. A man who had predicted Bengal's break from Pakistan in 1951 dismissed Urdu, the language Dhaka's students had died resisting as the sole state language, as no real national language in 1953.

## Limits of the record

![Illustration: an empty study with a desk lamp, a pipe, a red pencil and a string-tied archive box; on the wall, a map of the subcontinent with a blank patch where Bengal should be.](/images/stalin-bengal/empty-study.webp)
*What the archives have not yet given up. Illustration: AI-generated for The Fine Print.*

The forecast was a remark, not a policy. No document seen for this piece shows Moscow acting on it in Stalin's lifetime.

- **The forecast stayed in the room.** It is not in the CPI's 1951 Tactical Line, and I found no Indian or Bengali account of the meeting that reports it.
- **One American claim, unconfirmed.** A CIA bulletin of 23 March 1952 said Dange had received instructions to concentrate party work in eastern India and East Pakistan for joint guerrilla activity. No Soviet document seen here confirms this.
- **Second-hand sources.** The 1947 map remark comes through the Mitrokhin notes. The 1953 remarks rest on Menon's own accounts, the 1953 letter and his 1963 memoir; an American cable of 19 February 1953 confirms only that language was discussed.
- **The translation point** is settled by the original. The scan of sheet 75 has the future-tense «отпадут», as Kashin quotes it and as Singh translated it in 2006.

Still to examine:

1. The Soviet embassy in Karachi's reports on the February 1952 Dhaka events, if any, in the AVPRF Pakistan files (f. 0117). These are not online and need a visit to the archive in Moscow.
2. *The Mitrokhin Archive II*, p. 347, for the exact wording and source of the 1947 remark. The page is not available online; a 2007 Khaleej Times column renders the remark as "This country cannot last" but gives no page or source.
