**Plenty of big houses have sheltered refugees for a season. Number nine, Adams Road, in west Cambridge, did it for nearly a century.** From 1922, when a grieving widow built a room with two grand pianos for gathering people, until well into this century, three generations of one family kept its doors open to anyone who knocked: exiles from Franco's Spain and Pinochet's Chile, Arabs and Jews, Soviet dissidents, psychiatric patients judged too well to be locked up, students, strays. No charity ran it and nobody applied. It never became an institution. It stayed a home, with a visitors' book in which those who passed through wrote page after page.

Cambridge has mostly forgotten it. I knocked on its door once, for one night, in December 2005.

## Number nine

It was dark by the time I got there, and the houses had no numbers on them. I asked my way to Adams Road from person after person, and then found number six, which was not Rosemary's. I guessed at a house and rang the bell. I could not tell whether it had rung. There was no light at the front. I stood in the dark wondering whether to try the kitchen door round the side, when the front door opened.

A man put his head out. "Who's that?"

"Is it number nine?"

At once a woman's voice came from behind him: "Is that Achinti? Please come in."

I had been warned. That winter I was a translator-in-residence at the University of East Anglia in Norwich, on a Charles Wallace India Trust fellowship. I had come to Cambridge for a day and a half with very little money. "Then stay with Rosemary," Amanda had said. She directed the centre where I worked and had appointed herself my local guardian. "My cousin. It's a huge Edwardian pile. Mind you, the people in that house are all a bit, well, eccentric."

A single dim bulb was burning. Things were piled everywhere, and bookshelves stood stacked against each other. The man disappeared upstairs. "Let me show you your room," Rosemary said. "I'm very sorry, we live in very primitive conditions. And the heating isn't working." Of the two lamps by the bed, one worked. "It's very cold in here. Come and sit in the kitchen." I walked out leaving the light on. "Always switch off the light before leaving the room."

In the kitchen she cooked and asked what I did and how I was finding England. Was she a doctor, I asked. "Yes, but retired. Five generations of doctors in our family. My mother was a doctor too."

I knew almost nothing then of the family, or of what the house had seen. Rosemary's great-grandmother, Evelyn Hopkinson, had bought number nine in 1902, as a widow. In August 1898 her husband, the engineer John Hopkinson, had been killed with three of their children in a climbing accident at Arolla in the Swiss Alps. She moved to Cambridge to live next door to her surviving son, Bertram, a professor of engineering, and named the house Ellerslie. In 1918 Bertram too was killed, when the aircraft he was flying crashed in bad weather. In 1922 Evelyn added a new room, the Oak Room, with two grand pianos and a great many sofas and armchairs: a room for gathering people. That, from then on, was what the house did.

As we talked, the inner door swung half open, and a young woman put her face round it and stood looking at me. Then a dog as big as a tiger came in, entirely mild, and did not so much as sniff me. Then the man who had opened the door: a slightly wild air, a loud voice, a checked jacket torn through at both elbows. His name was Robin. He had lived in the house for many years. As soon as we were introduced he put a packet of crackers in front of me. "Have this." Then another. "And this." Then a third. "And this." Then a plate of home-made cheese. "And this." Rosemary went on cooking, smiling to herself.

"Amanda tells me you have a dairy?"

"We did once. The last cow went about forty years ago." During the Second World War, she said, a young woman from the Land Army had been sent to look after the cows. She had stayed on ever since. She was now ninety-seven. Her name was Frances.

That wartime farm was the work of Rosemary's mother, Dr Alice Roughton, Bertram's eldest daughter. Alice had married the physiologist Jack Roughton in 1925 and moved into number nine in 1933, when Evelyn died. In the war she turned the garden over to dairy cows, goats, pigs and poultry. She would live in the house until she died in 1995, and it was she who made it what it became.

## The visitors' book

Robin went out. A man who had been working around the house came in for his money, put his teacup down on the table, and reminded Rosemary. "Would you like some tea?" Yes, I said, with plenty of sugar. "You don't mind a tea bag? How do you make tea in India?"

Another young man came in through the kitchen door, groaning like a man with the weight of the world on his shoulders. "Oof. Big problem. She ignored me again today. I ran into her outside the library. She said hello, I said hello, and then a friend of mine turned up and she walked off."

"How long have you been stalking her?" asked Rosemary.

"Stalking?" He was outraged. "You call it stalking?" Then: "Two and a half weeks."

The moment he heard I was from Calcutta he said, in Bengali, "*Botol-ta khola jak*" (let's open the bottle). There was no bottle, of course. Then: "Are you *bhadralok*?" It turned out that a man named Parimal Ghosh had once lived in this house, researching Calcutta and its *bhadralok*, the educated Bengali middle class. Everyone spoke of him as an extraordinary person. When had he been here? "Wait," said Rosemary, "let me look in the visitors' book."

Her mother had started it, she said, to keep the names and addresses of everyone who stayed. "But look how much people have written." Page after page, some of them. One volume had filled while her mother was alive; many more since. We found Parimal, five years earlier. He had had polio as a child and used a wheelchair, and his girlfriend had found this house for him when his lodgings proved impossible. (Back in Calcutta, Parimal Ghosh corrected me on two points: she was a friend, not a girlfriend, and he had come on a Charles Wallace fellowship, like me, not for a PhD.)

That book was a record of something remarkable. Alice Roughton, a GP and psychiatrist, had taken her vocation further than most. Her obituary in the *Independent* records that when she judged a borstal boy or a psychotic patient did not truly need locking up, she brought them home to live with her. Then came the refugees. In June 1939 the Catalan composer Roberto Gerhard and his wife Poldi reached Cambridge as exiles from Franco's Spain; Alice gave them money and a roof, and Gerhard later dedicated his Wind Quintet to her. After the war came others from wherever the century was cruellest: Arabs alongside Jews, Soviet anti-communists beside Latin American revolutionaries, a Chilean family after the 1973 coup. Every Sunday evening the house was open to anyone, on the principle that no quarrel could not be talked out around the fire. She helped found the Medical Association for the Prevention of War in 1951, and anti-nuclear campaigners met regularly at her house.

The lovesick young man was Rory. How long had he lived here? "As long as I can remember." He was doing a PhD in Latin American literature, and loved Gregory Rabassa, Márquez's translator. Had I read *Hopscotch*? "You read a bit forward, then jump back." And *One Hundred Years of Solitude*? At once he began reciting it from the first line, paragraph after paragraph, like a poem, laying the table for supper all the while.

Then the door crashed open and a tall young man came tumbling in. "Sorry, I'm late." His name was Tom. Later that night Rosemary told me, unconvinced, that he was trying to unite relativity and gravity in a single theory, through calculation after calculation. So far, he had not managed.

## Supper

By now everyone had arrived. Robin was back. Frances had come in, tiny and ancient. Rory, Tom, the young woman from the doorway, and Sunny the dog. Another young woman came in too, whose English was not perfect. She waited tables in a restaurant and had been in trouble with the owners for eating the food ("often," she said later). Her name was Kasia (?). She was from Poland, doing an MA in English and paying for it with restaurant shifts.

"Help yourselves," said Rosemary. I took a roast potato from the oven, a little stewed red cabbage from an enormous pot, and some tinned tuna turned out into a bowl.

The young woman from the doorway was Cathy. "I am Cathy," she told me. "Dr Summers' daughter." She pointed. "I am her daughter." She was perhaps twenty-five. She had Down syndrome, Amanda told me later, and understood almost everything. Rosemary had also had two sons. Both had died young. Since then she had been taking in young people who came from elsewhere to study.

We were eating when the kitchen door opened again and a young man with long golden hair came in, Kasia's boyfriend, I thought, come for his English lesson. Rosemary scolded Kasia: "Why didn't you ask him to supper?" His name was Radek. He had come from Poland looking for work and did odd jobs: mending someone's fence one day, clearing someone's drains the next. He had brought his own food in a paper bundle, and went off somewhere to eat it. Cathy announced, "Boyfriend. I very much like boyfriends." Everyone said, "OK, OK."

When Frances first came in, Rory had been rolling a cigarette. Could one smoke here, I had asked. "What can't you do here? You could smoke a joint if you wanted." Frances walked in, took hold of Rory's ear and twisted it, hard. "When are you going to stop all this? You'd be much healthier if you did." "I am healthy," said Rory, and yawned. Another tremendous twist. "Healthy? Sitting there yawning, and you say you're healthy?"

One by one the others drifted off, some washing their own plates. Rosemary, Cathy, Radek, Kasia and I stayed at the table. Cathy and Rory had made a tiramisu the day before. Kasia could not make up her mind, so Cathy put her palms together and begged: "Please, please, I will be very happy if you have it." It was good.

Kasia asked about India; I asked about Poland. She had been too small under socialism to understand, she said, but people said there had been no food, that it all went to Russia. "Don't say there was no food," said Rosemary. "I lived in Poland for a time. There was food. But the food was boring. And don't forget that everyone got to eat."

Then Kasia asked me, "Do you believe in God?" No, I said. "I believe in God, but I don't believe in the Church." Radek, in broken English: "But church is house of God."

"Trust is a gift," said Rosemary. "You cannot earn it. You can lose it, but you cannot acquire it. If you don't have trust, there's nothing you can do. But then you don't even trust that you will go to hell." Then she described an experiment in which four people are told to give the wrong answer about the length of some lines, and the fifth, who answers correctly at first, ends up agreeing with them. I nearly said that Goebbels had worked on much the same theory, but stopped myself in time.

Radek had found no work that day. "I may not always remember," said Rosemary, "but you must keep coming and asking me. A leg has come off one of my beds. Come and fix it tomorrow. And the leaves on the roof need clearing. Come in the morning." "Eight?" "All right."

The kitchen was the only warm room in the house, so Kasia would teach Radek there. I put out my light at ten but could not sleep. In the end I got hungry and ate some biscuits from my bag. When I went down, the lesson was over. I must have fallen asleep at about two.

## Morning

At a quarter past eight Rosemary tapped on my door. The kitchen was empty; nobody else was up. "Porridge? Or bread? Or cereal?" The last seemed safest.

Frances came in while I was eating and told me about her life. She had been hard of hearing since childhood, so she could not join the Army or the Air Force as she had wanted; she signed up for the Land Army instead, and it brought her here. She had once smoked heavily herself, she said. At sixteen she fell ill, and a doctor told her that if she didn't stop at once she wouldn't see next Christmas. She went home and threw every cigarette into the fire. She still intended to live a long time.

Rory came in. "*Botol-ta khola jak*," he said, and helped himself to porridge. He had a friend whose parents had married at Rajneesh's ashram in Pune, he told me, and named their son Sangam: the meeting of two rivers. I told him *sangam* had another meaning too. He was thrilled. When Rosemary came in he said, "Do you know, Sangam means copulation?" "Then it's exactly the right name for him," said Rosemary.

Rosemary gave me the visitors' book to write my name and address, and I wrote a few lines in it as well. When she read them her eyes nearly filled, and she said again and again, "So kind of you, so kind of you."

Rory would not be going to the library today, he said, in case he ran into that girl and wrecked his whole day's work. Was he muttering her name? "Don't be silly, I'd have to know it first. But she's gorgeous." Radek had already finished some of the carpentry. Kasia came down on her way to the university. I went out to the Centre of South Asian Studies and the Fitzwilliam Museum, and promised to be back for lunch.

## Lunch, and a coat of arms with pigs

I got back at nearly one, by the kitchen door. Robin came in and handed me a packet of crackers. "Have this." He had been a solicitor, Rosemary told me, and was now thinking about what to do next. Then Radek, Kasia and Rory. Frances came in out of breath, a badge pinned to her navy-blue jacket: PLEASE SPEAK CLEARLY. Soup on the stove, buns, butter and cheese on the table.

Over lunch Robin questioned me. What was I working on? Had I done any reporting myself? How many wives did I have at home? ("So far only one," I said. Rosemary caught it. "Mind you, he said *so far*.") How many children? Fifty? Sixty? More?

It was a merry meal. Afterwards I photographed Frances ("Frances is very photogenic," said Rosemary), and Rory fetched his own camera and took pictures of us all, among them the one of Rosemary and me.

Above the kitchen door hung a painting: a coat of arms like a British monarch's, except that instead of a lion and a unicorn, the shield was held up by two pigs. Rosemary's mother had commissioned it, she said. The family had once owned a Rolls-Royce, and Alice used to drive it to fetch swill for the household's pigs, carrying it in a dustbin. A Rolls-Royce put to such use, she decided, ought to carry a coat of arms on its bonnet. So an artist painted two copies: one for the car, one for over the kitchen door.

The shield is white on the left and red on the right, with a black band across the red half. The family began out of wedlock, said Rosemary, and the band records that. One of the first couple came from Yorkshire and the other from Lancashire: the white rose and the red. Every part of the family's story, she said, was told in the arms somehow.

I washed some plates, said my goodbyes, and set off for the station.

## What I wrote in the visitors' book

As best I remember it, and not a word of it invented:

> 'The heating system is not working,' I was told soon after I had stepped into the house. But during my few hours' stay here, never did I feel that the house was lacking in warmth.
>
> The great Indian poet, Rabindranath Tagore, after his visit to the Soviet Russia in the 1930s, had written: 'Had I not been to Russia, my pilgrimage to life would have remained incomplete.' I would also like to say that had I not been to this place, my pilgrimage to England would have remained incomplete. I wouldn't have known that people like the Summers still existed in this selfish little village called 'the world'.

Copying it out years later, it struck me as terribly sentimental. But that was how it felt at the time.

## The bus stop

That night I am back in Norwich, at a bus stop outside the station. It is late and the last bus to the university has not come. The glass wall of the shelter is smashed, and the wind comes howling through it, carrying rain. I am shivering so hard I can barely breathe.

There are two other people at the stop, a young man and a young woman. Students, probably. The young man points at me and says to the girl, "Paki, Paki." The two of them double up laughing. Lacking the will, the strength and the courage to object, I neither hear nor see any of it.

When the bus comes, a voice beside me says, "You're soaked through." It is Sylvie, from my own department. I try to smile, and find that even the smile has frozen. Back in my flat I make hot soup, drink half a bottle of brandy, and sit down to write a letter home. One day and one night, and no shortage of experiences.

## Afterwards

What I had walked into, I later learned, was the tail end of that century. Alice died there on 16 June 1995, aged ninety, nursed by Rosemary after a stroke. In all, Rosemary ran the house for thirty-five years, her brother Geoffrey wrote in her *Guardian* obituary, for "anyone who had become a little lost in the hurly-burly of life".

In May 2008 Rosemary gave Roberto Gerhard's archive to Cambridge University Library, so the exile her mother sheltered in 1939 is now kept in the city for good. A Catalan writer, Xavier Muñoz Puiggròs, who first met Alice as a student in 1978, has written a whole biography of her around the house.

Rosemary has died, at eighty-five. So have Cathy, Frances and Robin. Former residents keep a Facebook group, "I lived at 9 Adams Road, Cambridge (Number 9)". The house was put up for sale around 2022, a magnificent Edwardian pile "requiring modernisation", and has since been sold. Whoever lives there now, I hope the kitchen is still warm.
